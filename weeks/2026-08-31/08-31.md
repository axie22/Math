## 2. Core (~35 min) — Induction I

*Read `lessons/proof-foundations.md` §6 first if the mechanism isn't already clear
from the calibration feedback — this is the first time that section has actually been
needed. It has one worked example (a sum of squares) and the one sentence that
matters most: if your inductive step doesn't visibly use the hypothesis, you haven't
done induction.*

### Core 1 — sum formula, straight application

**Prove by induction:** for all $n\ge1$,
$$1+3+5+\cdots+(2n-1) = n^2.$$

Before you write the inductive step, answer in one line: **what is $S(n)$ here, and
what is $S(n+1)$ in terms of $S(n)$?** (This is the Σ-reading check — get this wrong
and the whole step is built on sand, same failure mode as the calibration's A2.) Then
do the two parts:

- **Base case** ($n=1$).
- **Inductive step.** State the induction hypothesis explicitly, as its own labeled
  line. Then show $S(n+1)=S(n)+(2(n+1)-1)$ and substitute the hypothesis in — don't
  reprove $S(n+1)$ from scratch.

### Core 2 — divisibility, a genuinely different shape

**Prove by induction:** for all $n\ge1$, $3\mid(n^3-n)$.

This isn't a running sum, so $S(n+1)=S(n)+a_{n+1}$ doesn't apply directly — you'll
need to **unfold what "$3\mid(n^3-n)$" means** (§1 of `proof-foundations.md`: it means
$n^3-n=3m$ for some integer $m$) and use *that* equation as your hypothesis when you
expand $(n+1)^3-(n+1)$. Same engine, different fuel — this is the point of pairing it
with Core 1: induction isn't "the sum-formula technique," it's "build $n+1$ out of
$n$ and substitute," and that works on divisibility claims exactly as well as on
sums.

Concretely: expand $(n+1)^3-(n+1)$, group it into $\bigl(n^3-n\bigr)+(\text{leftover
terms})$, substitute the induction hypothesis for the $n^3-n$ piece, and show the
whole thing is $3\times(\text{an integer})$.

## Stretch (~15 min, optional, fine to skip)

**Prove by induction:** $n!>2^n$ for all $n\ge4$.

*(Note the base case isn't $n=1$ — check $n=1,2,3$ yourself and notice the claim is
actually false there. Induction proves "for all $n\ge n_0$," and $n_0$ doesn't have
to be the smallest value the statement is written for; it has to be wherever the
statement first becomes true. Starting the base case at the wrong place is a common,
quiet way to build a correct-looking proof of a true claim on top of a false base
case.)*
