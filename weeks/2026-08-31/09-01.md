## 1. Repair (~10 min) — plain-English quantifier meaning, worked example first

**Worked example.** Let $R(x,y)$ be "$x<y$" over $\mathbb{R}$.

$\forall x\,\exists y\,R(x,y)$ is **true**: pick any real $x$; then $y=x+1$ works, so
a bigger number always exists. In words, this claims *there is no largest real
number* — for every candidate $x$, something bigger can always be found, and which
$y$ works depends on which $x$ you started with.

$\exists y\,\forall x\,R(x,y)$ is **false**: this would require *one single* $y$
that is bigger than every real number $x$ at once — including $y+1$ itself. No such
$y$ exists. That's the whole content of "there is no largest real number," said the
other way around.

**Your turn.** Let $T(x,y)$ be "$x-y=1$" over $\mathbb{R}$.

**(a)** True or false, no proof needed: $\forall x\,\exists y\,T(x,y)$? $\exists
y\,\forall x\,T(x,y)$?

**(b)** In a sentence each, modeled on the worked example above: what does
$\forall x\,\exists y\,T(x,y)$ **claim**? (What is $y$ in terms of $x$ — does it
change as $x$ changes, and how?) What single fixed number would $y$ have to be for
$\exists y\,\forall x\,T(x,y)$ to hold, and why does no real number work?

## 2. Core (~35 min) — Induction II: two new representations

*Yesterday's engine — build $n+1$ out of $n$, substitute the hypothesis — wasn't
specific to sums. Today shows it applied to counting and to a genuinely different
inductive structure (strong induction).*

### Core 1 — counting, not summing: a set of size $n$ has $2^n$ subsets

**Prove by induction:** for all $n\ge0$, a set of size $n$ has exactly $2^n$
subsets.

This is **literally item 2 of the Phase 0 exit gate** in `CURRICULUM.md` — worth
doing carefully now rather than cold under time pressure later.

There's no sum recurrence here ($S(n+1)=S(n)+a_{n+1}$ doesn't apply — you're not
adding a term to a running total), so the hypothesis has to enter differently.
Before writing the proof, answer in one line: **given a set $A$ of size $n+1$ and
one element $x\in A$, how does every subset of $A$ relate to subsets of
$A\setminus\{x\}$** (the size-$n$ set your hypothesis is about)? Split subsets of
$A$ into two groups by whether they contain $x$ or not, count each group using the
hypothesis, and add.

- **Base case** ($n=0$): the empty set.
- **Inductive step.** State the hypothesis explicitly (a set of size $n$ has $2^n$
  subsets). Use the split above to show a set of size $n+1$ has $2\cdot2^n=2^{n+1}$
  subsets.

### Core 2 — strong induction: every integer $n\ge2$ is a product of primes

**Prove by induction:** every integer $n\ge2$ can be written as a product of one or
more primes.

`lessons/proof-foundations.md` §6 already sketches *why* ordinary induction doesn't
give you this one directly — read that short paragraph (end of §6, "Strong
induction") before starting, it's four sentences. The problem: $n+1$ might factor as
$a\cdot b$ where neither $a$ nor $b$ is $n$ — ordinary induction only hands you the
statement at $n$, not at $a$ and $b$, which could be much smaller. **Strong
induction** assumes the statement holds for *every* integer from $2$ up to $n$, not
just at $n$ itself.

- **Base case** ($n=2$): trivial — say why.
- **Inductive step.** Assume every integer $m$ with $2\le m\le n$ can be written as
  a product of primes (the **strong** hypothesis — note it covers a range, not one
  value). Show $n+1$ can be. Split into two cases: $n+1$ is prime (done immediately
  — say why), or $n+1$ is composite, so $n+1=ab$ with $1<a,b\le n$ (say why $a,b\le
  n$, not just $a,b<n+1$) — then apply the hypothesis to $a$ and to $b$ separately
  and combine.

This connects back to R1 of yesterday's session ($\sqrt5$'s proof used "5 is prime
$\Rightarrow$ $5\mid p^2 \Rightarrow 5\mid p$") — primality has been doing real work
in this repo for two weeks; today you prove the fact (every integer factors into
primes) that makes "prime factorization" a well-defined thing to talk about at all.
