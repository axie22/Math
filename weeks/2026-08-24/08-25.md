## 1. Connectives under negation (~15 min) — the repair item, third pass

Two sessions in a row (08-20, 08-21) this failed the same way: the arrow surviving,
the antecedent getting negated instead of asserted, or De Morgan applied to only one
side. The rule, stated once more:

$$\lnot(A \implies B) \;\equiv\; A \wedge \lnot B \qquad\qquad \lnot(A\wedge B)\equiv\lnot A\vee\lnot B \qquad \lnot(A\vee B)\equiv\lnot A\wedge\lnot B$$

The arrow **disappears**. The antecedent is **asserted, not negated**. "And"/"or"
**swap**, and **both sides get negated**. Domain restrictions ($\varepsilon>0$,
$n\in\mathbb{Z}$, etc.) are **never** touched — that's a separate rule, and 08-20's (d)
showed it can resurface even once the connective rule is solid, so today deliberately
mixes both in the same problem instead of testing them apart.

Negate each. **Name the rule before writing the answer.**

**(a)** $\forall t \in \mathbb{R},\ \big(t \ge 5 \implies 2t - 1 \ge 9\big)$

**(b)** $\exists k \in \mathbb{Z},\ \big(k \text{ is odd} \wedge k < -50\big)$

**(c)** $\forall \delta > 0,\ \big(|x| < \delta \implies x^2 < \delta\big)$
*(one restriction, one implication — same shape as the ε-δ statements coming in
Phase 0's exit gate)*

**(d) — restriction and connective together, the trap from 08-20.** $\forall M > 0,\
\exists n \in \mathbb{N},\ \big(n > M \implies a_n < 0\big)$. *Three separate things
happen here: one quantifier flips, one stays put as $\exists\to\forall$, one
restriction never changes, and the implication becomes a conjunction. Do all four
correctly in one pass.*

**(e) — a trap, read-back only.** Negate $\exists x \in \mathbb{R},\ (x > 0 \vee x = 0)$.
Then say whether your negation is true or false, and why, in one line.

## 2. NEW — using a negation to prove something diverges (~20 min)

**(a)** Write $\lnot(a_n \to L)$ for the standard definition
$\forall\varepsilon>0,\exists N\in\mathbb{N},\forall n>N,\ |a_n-L|<\varepsilon$.

**(b)** Read it as a to-do list: three sentences, in order — what you must *choose*,
what you must *accept as arbitrary*, what you must *construct*.

**(c)** Prove: **$a_n = 2 + (-1)^n$ does not converge to $2$.**
*This one's a trap of a different kind — $2$ is the "obvious" average of the sequence's
two values, $1$ and $3$. Averages are not limits. Pick your $\varepsilon$, take an
arbitrary $N$, construct an $n>N$, verify $|a_n - 2|\ge\varepsilon$ with real numbers.*

**(d)** Now the stronger claim: **$a_n = 2+(-1)^n$ converges to no $L$ at all.** Your
$\varepsilon$ can't depend on $L$ this time. *Hint: consecutive terms are always
distance $2$ apart — same fact you'll want for (c) and (d) both, just used once for a
fixed candidate and once for an arbitrary one.*

## 3. NEW — the order of the quantifiers (~15 min)

**(a)** Let $P(x,y)$ be "$x + y = 0$" over $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$. One of
$\forall x\exists y\,P(x,y)$ and $\exists y\forall x\,P(x,y)$ is true, the other false.
Say which, and prove both — the true one by constructing $y$ from $x$, the false one
by negating it and exhibiting a counterexample.

**(b)** One plain-English sentence each: what does $\forall x\exists y\,P(x,y)$ assert
about the reals, and what would $\exists y\forall x\,P(x,y)$ have asserted if it were
true?

**(c)** Continuity of $f$ at $a$ is
$\forall\varepsilon>0,\exists\delta>0,(|x-a|<\delta\implies|f(x)-f(a)|<\varepsilon)$ —
$\delta$ is allowed to depend on $\varepsilon$. Suppose instead someone swapped the
order:
$$\exists\delta>0,\ \forall\varepsilon>0,\ \big(|x-a|<\delta\implies|f(x)-f(a)|<\varepsilon\big).$$
What would this altered statement force to be true about $f$ near $a$? *(Think about
what it means for $|f(x)-f(a)|$ to be less than **every** positive $\varepsilon$ at
once, for all $x$ within that one fixed $\delta$.)*
