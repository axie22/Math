## 1. Repair (~8 min) — plain English, not symbol transliteration

Yesterday's §3(b) asked what $\forall x\exists y\,P(x,y)$ and $\exists y\forall x\,P(x,y)$ *assert*, and got back "for all x there exists a y" — a restatement of the symbols, not an explanation of what they mean about the actual objects involved.

Let $R(x,y)$ be "$y=x^2$" over $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$.

**(a)** Is $\forall x\exists y\,R(x,y)$ true or false? Is $\exists y\forall x\,R(x,y)$ true or false? (No proof needed — you did this kind of construction correctly yesterday. Just state which is which.)

**(b)** Now the actual ask: in a sentence each, say what $\forall x\exists
y\,R(x,y)$ **claims about real numbers** (not "for all x there exists a y" — what
*is* the y, in words?), and say what $\exists y\forall x\,R(x,y)$ **would have had
to be true** for it to hold (what would that one $y$ have to be, simultaneously, for
every $x$?), and why no real number can do that.

## 2. Core (~35 min) — new material

### Core 1 — Taylor's theorem, with a required proof

**(a)** Write the degree-2 Taylor polynomial $T_2(x)$ for $f(x)=e^x$ at $a=0$, and
write the Lagrange remainder $R_2(x)$ explicitly (in terms of an unknown $c$).

**(b) — proof required.** Using $R_2(x)$, **prove** that the degree-2 approximation
of $e^x$ is accurate to within $\frac{e}{6}$ for every $x\in[0,1]$ — i.e., prove
$|R_2(x)|\le\frac{e}{6}$ on that interval. You'll need a bound on $e^c$ for $c$
between $0$ and $x\in[0,1]$; state it and justify it in one line.

### Core 2 — geometric series, connected to this week's $\varepsilon$-$N$ work

**(a)** Derive $S_n=\sum_{k=0}^n r^k = \frac{1-r^{n+1}}{1-r}$ using the
multiply-and-subtract trick from the lesson (not induction — that's Thursday's
topic). Show the cancellation explicitly.

**(b)** Using (a) and citing (without reproving) that $r^{n+1}\to0$ when $|r|<1$,
write $\sum_{k=0}^\infty r^k$ in closed form.

**(c) — proof required, and the connection to this week.** Prove directly from the
$\varepsilon$-$N$ definition that $\left(\frac12\right)^n \to 0$. (You'll need to
produce an explicit $N$ in terms of $\varepsilon$ — a logarithm is the natural tool,
but state clearly why the $N$ you pick works for every $n>N$.)
