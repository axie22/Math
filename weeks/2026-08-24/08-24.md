## 1. Connectives under negation (~15 min) — the repair item

The rule you're missing, stated once:

$$\lnot(A \implies B) \;\equiv\; A \wedge \lnot B$$

The arrow **disappears**. The antecedent $A$ is *asserted*, not negated — you're claiming
a witness that **satisfies the hypothesis and fails the conclusion.** And by De Morgan,
$\lnot(A \wedge B) \equiv \lnot A \vee \lnot B$, $\lnot(A \vee B) \equiv \lnot A \wedge \lnot B$ — the connective flips *and both sides get negated.*

Negate each. For every one, **name the rule you applied before writing the answer.**
Domain restrictions stay exactly as written.

**(a)** $\forall n \in \mathbb{Z},\ \big(n > 5 \implies n^2 > 25\big)$

**(b)** $\exists x \in \mathbb{R},\ \big(x \ge 1 \wedge x \le 0\big)$

**(c)** $\forall f,\ \big(f \text{ differentiable} \implies f \text{ continuous}\big)$

**(d)** $\forall x \in \mathbb{R},\ \big(x \in \mathbb{Q} \vee x \in \mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}\big)$

**(e)** $\forall \varepsilon > 0,\ \exists \delta > 0,\ \big(|x - a| < \delta \implies |f(x) - f(a)| < \varepsilon\big)$

*(e) is the definition of continuity at $a$ — three quantifiers and an implication at the
bottom. Two restrictions to leave alone, two quantifiers to flip, one arrow to destroy.*

**(f) — a trap.** Negate $\exists n \in \mathbb{N},\ \big(n \text{ is prime} \wedge n > 10^6\big)$. Then say whether your negation is true or false, and why. *(One line. This is checking that you can still read what a negation means, not just produce it.)*

## 2. NEW — using a negation to prove something diverges (~20 min)

Producing a negation is half the skill. The half that matters is *using* one as a proof
obligation: the negation tells you exactly what you must exhibit.

Convergence: $a_n \to L$ means $\forall \varepsilon > 0,\ \exists N \in \mathbb{N},\ \forall n > N,\ |a_n - L| < \varepsilon$.

**(a)** Write $\lnot(a_n \to L)$. You did this correctly on the calibration retest — the
point here is what comes next.

**(b)** Read your negation as a **to-do list.** It starts $\exists \varepsilon > 0$, so
your proof must start by *choosing* a specific $\varepsilon$. Then $\forall N$, so you
must handle an arbitrary $N$. Then $\exists n > N$, so you must *construct* an $n$ from
that $N$. Write out those three obligations as three sentences before proving anything.

**(c)** Now prove it: **$a_n = (-1)^n$ does not converge to $0$.**
Pick your $\varepsilon$, take an arbitrary $N$, produce an $n > N$, and verify
$|a_n - 0| \ge \varepsilon$ with actual arithmetic.

**(d)** Now the stronger claim: **$a_n = (-1)^n$ does not converge to *any* $L$.**
One extra quantifier out front ($\forall L \in \mathbb{R}$), so your $\varepsilon$ may no
longer depend on knowing $L$. *Hint: consecutive terms are always distance 2 apart, and
they can't both be within $\varepsilon$ of the same $L$ if $\varepsilon$ is small enough.*

## 3. NEW — the order of the quantifiers (~15 min)

$$\forall x\, \exists y\, P(x,y) \qquad\text{vs.}\qquad \exists y\, \forall x\, P(x,y)$$

In the first, $y$ may depend on $x$. In the second, one $y$ must work for every $x$ at
once. The second is strictly stronger, and this distinction is the entire difference
between "converges" and "converges uniformly," which is half the theorem statements in
analysis.

**(a)** Let $P(x,y)$ be "$y > x$" over $x, y \in \mathbb{R}$. One of
$\forall x \exists y\, P$ and $\exists y \forall x\, P$ is true and the other is false.
Say which, and **prove both claims** — the true one by constructing $y$ from $x$, the
false one by negating it and exhibiting a counterexample.

**(b)** In one sentence each, in plain English, what do the two statements in (a)
actually assert about the real numbers?

**(c)** Go back to the convergence definition. $N$ appears after $\varepsilon$, so $N$
may depend on $\varepsilon$. What would the definition mean if you swapped them to
$\exists N,\ \forall \varepsilon > 0,\ \forall n > N,\ |a_n - L| < \varepsilon$? Is any
sequence at all like that? *(Yes — describe which ones.)*
