# Day 4 Solutions — Multivariable Calculus (advanced)

## Problem 1 (warm-up): Critical points and the Hessian test

**What's being asked:** find where $f(x,y) = x^2 - 4x + y^2 + 2y + 5$ has zero gradient, then use the Hessian to classify each such point as a min, max, or saddle. This is exactly yesterday's technique (Section 3 of the lesson) — we're warming back up before adding constraints in Problems 2 and 3.

**Step 1 — find critical points.** Compute the gradient:

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2x - 4, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 2y + 2$$

Set both to zero: $2x-4=0 \Rightarrow x=2$, and $2y+2=0 \Rightarrow y=-1$. So there is exactly **one** critical point, $(2,-1)$. (Since both partials are linear in a single variable each, there's no way to get a second solution — unlike yesterday's cubic examples, which had multiple critical points.)

**Step 2 — build the Hessian.** Second partials:

$$f_{xx} = 2, \qquad f_{yy} = 2, \qquad f_{xy} = f_{yx} = 0$$

$$H = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$$

**Step 3 — apply the second derivative test.** $D = \det(H) = (2)(2) - 0^2 = 4 > 0$, and $f_{xx} = 2 > 0$. By the test (Section 3 of the lesson): $D>0$ and $f_{xx}>0$ means every principal direction of curvature is positive — the surface bends upward no matter which way you look — so this is a **local minimum**.

**Step 4 — the minimum value, with a sanity check.** Plug in: $f(2,-1) = (2)^2 - 4(2) + (-1)^2 + 2(-1) + 5 = 4 - 8 + 1 - 2 + 5 = 0$.

Sanity check by completing the square: $x^2-4x = (x-2)^2 - 4$ and $y^2+2y=(y+1)^2-1$, so

$$f(x,y) = (x-2)^2 - 4 + (y+1)^2 - 1 + 5 = (x-2)^2 + (y+1)^2$$

This is manifestly a sum of two squares — a perfect "bowl" shape with minimum value $0$ exactly at $(2,-1)$. That matches the Hessian test exactly, and it's a nice reminder that the Hessian test is really just a shortcut for "can I complete the square (in every direction at once)?"

$$\boxed{\text{Local minimum at } (2,-1), \text{ with } f(2,-1) = 0}$$

## Problem 2 (core): Lagrange multipliers, two variables

**What's being asked:** minimize $f(x,y) = x^2+y^2$ but *only* among points on the line $x+y=6$ — not the whole plane. Unconstrained calculus (setting $\nabla f = 0$) would just give $x=y=0$, which isn't on the line at all, so it's the wrong tool here. We need Lagrange multipliers (Section 4 of the lesson): at the constrained optimum, $\nabla f$ must be parallel to $\nabla g$, where $g(x,y)=x+y$.

**Step 1 — set up the gradients.**

$$\nabla f = (2x,\ 2y), \qquad \nabla g = (1,\ 1)$$

**Step 2 — write the Lagrange condition $\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$.** Component-wise:

$$2x = \lambda \cdot 1 = \lambda, \qquad 2y = \lambda \cdot 1 = \lambda$$

Since both equal $\lambda$, they equal each other: $2x = 2y \Rightarrow x = y$. Geometrically, this says the point where the circle $x^2+y^2=\text{const}$ touches the line $x+y=6$ must lie exactly on the diagonal $x=y$ — which makes sense, since the line $x+y=6$ has slope $-1$ and is symmetric under swapping $x \leftrightarrow y$, so its closest point to the origin (in the sense of smallest $x^2+y^2$) should be the symmetric point.

**Step 3 — bring in the constraint.** We still need the third equation, $g(x,y)=6$: substitute $x=y$ into $x+y=6$:

$$2x = 6 \Rightarrow x = 3, \quad y = 3$$

And $\lambda = 2x = 6$.

**Step 4 — evaluate and sanity-check.** $f(3,3) = 9+9=18$.

Geometric check (same reasoning as the lesson's micro-example): the level curve $x^2+y^2=18$ is a circle of radius $\sqrt{18}\approx 4.24$ centered at the origin. The line $x+y=6$ is tangent to this circle exactly at $(3,3)$ — try any smaller circle and it never reaches the line; try a bigger one and it crosses the line at two points instead of touching it once. Minimality corresponds exactly to "the smallest circle that still reaches the line," i.e., tangency.

There's also a slicker way to see this is right: the closest point on a line $x+y=c$ to the origin is always $\left(\frac{c}{2},\frac{c}{2}\right)$ by symmetry, and the squared distance is $2\left(\frac{c}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{c^2}{2}$. With $c=6$: $\frac{36}{2}=18$. Matches.

$$\boxed{(x,y) = (3,3), \quad \lambda = 6, \quad f_{\min} = 18}$$

## Problem 3 (stretch): Lagrange multipliers, three variables — minimum-energy thrust allocation

**What's being asked:** this is the same technique as Problem 2, but now in three variables, and dressed in an aerospace-flavored story: three thrusters must jointly produce $90\text{ N}$ of total thrust ($u_1+u_2+u_3=90$), and we want to choose the split that minimizes total squared effort $f(u_1,u_2,u_3)=u_1^2+u_2^2+u_3^2$ — a common proxy for power dissipation/heat generated by a control system, since resistive/actuator losses typically scale with the *square* of the commanded output, not the output itself. This is exactly the kind of "minimum-energy control allocation" problem that shows up in real spacecraft attitude control when you have redundant actuators.

**Step 1 — set up the gradients.** With $g(u_1,u_2,u_3) = u_1+u_2+u_3$:

$$\nabla f = (2u_1,\ 2u_2,\ 2u_3), \qquad \nabla g = (1,\ 1,\ 1)$$

The Lagrange machinery generalizes to $n$ variables exactly as stated in the lesson: it's still "gradient of $f$ parallel to gradient of $g$," just with more components.

**Step 2 — write $\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$ component-wise.**

$$2u_1 = \lambda, \qquad 2u_2 = \lambda, \qquad 2u_3 = \lambda$$

All three equal the same $\lambda$, so $u_1 = u_2 = u_3$. This is the key qualitative result, and it's worth pausing on *why* it's true before even finishing the algebra: because the cost $u_1^2+u_2^2+u_3^2$ treats all three thrusters completely symmetrically (swapping any two labels doesn't change $f$ or $g$), the optimum has to be symmetric too — there's no reason for the optimizer to prefer thruster 1 over thruster 2. This symmetry argument is a fast sanity check you can do *before* grinding through algebra, and it generalizes: whenever you minimize a sum of squares subject to a fixed total, equal shares are optimal (this is the same fact underlying why variance is minimized by equal allocation, and it's related to the Cauchy–Schwarz / power-mean inequality).

**Step 3 — apply the constraint.** $u_1+u_2+u_3=90$ with $u_1=u_2=u_3=u$ gives $3u=90 \Rightarrow u=30$. So $u_1=u_2=u_3=30\text{ N}$, and $\lambda = 2u = 60$.

**Step 4 — compute the minimum energy value.**

$$f(30,30,30) = 30^2+30^2+30^2 = 3(900) = 2700$$

**Step 5 — interpret.** The optimal allocation is *perfectly even*: each thruster contributes exactly one-third of the total commanded thrust. This is a genuinely useful engineering fact, not just a coincidence of the numbers chosen: for identical actuators with quadratic cost, minimum-energy control allocation always splits the load equally, and any uneven split (say $u_1=90, u_2=u_3=0$) costs strictly more energy — check: $90^2+0+0=8100 \gg 2700$. That's why, when actuators are interchangeable, spacecraft and aircraft control allocation logic defaults toward even load-sharing unless some other constraint (an actuator near its limit, a fault, an asymmetric cost) forces an uneven split. The multiplier $\lambda=60$ also has a concrete meaning here: it's the marginal energy cost, in $(\text{N}^2)/\text{N}$ units, of demanding one more Newton of total thrust from the system — relaxing the $90\text{ N}$ budget to $91\text{ N}$ would raise the minimum energy by approximately $\lambda = 60$.

$$\boxed{u_1=u_2=u_3=30\text{ N}, \quad \lambda=60, \quad f_{\min}=2700}$$

## Takeaway

Problem 1 confirmed the Hessian test still works exactly as it did yesterday; Problems 2 and 3 showed that adding a constraint doesn't require new calculus, just one new idea — tangency means parallel gradients — which scales cleanly from two variables to three (to, eventually, millions, as in constrained neural network training). The equal-split result in Problem 3 is not a toy fact: it's the same reasoning that governs load-balancing across parallel servers, power-splitting across redundant thrusters, and even weight decay's effect on network parameters.
