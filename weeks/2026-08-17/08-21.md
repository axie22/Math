*Combines two things learned on different days: the general pigeonhole principle
(2026-08-20) and the standard witness that injective doesn't imply surjective on an

**3a.** Prove the general pigeonhole principle you stated in 2d: for finite sets $A,B$
with $|A|>|B|$, no injective function $f:A\to B$ exists. (This generalizes Thursday's
$\{1,\dots,6\}\to\{1,\dots,5\}$ argument — same idea, arbitrary finite sizes instead
of the specific numbers 6 and 5.)

**3b.** Explain, in a real paragraph and not just a restatement, why this argument
*requires* finiteness — specifically, walk through what goes wrong if you try to run
the same counting argument on $f:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}$, $f(n)=2n$ (injective, not
surjective, domain and codomain both infinite and even the "same size" in the
cardinality sense). What is it about infinite sets that breaks the "$n$ distinct
