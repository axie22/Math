### 2d — pigeonhole, general principle (words first)

The pigeonhole principle states if there are more pigeons than there are boxes, then at least one box must hold more than one pigeon. This means that in the case of Thursday's problem where there were more inputs than outputs, having a function that is injective was impossible since the domain was larger than the codomain. ## Part 3 — Synthesis

### 3a — prove: finite $A,B$ with $|A|>|B|$ ⟹ no injective $f:A\to B$

Let there be, for the sake of contradiction, an injective function $f : A \rightarrow B$ with $|A| > |B|$. Let $|B| = n$. Then we know that $|A|$ must be at least $n + 1$ since $|A| > |B|$. 
We are trying to map $n + 1$ elements to $n$ slots. By the pigeonhole principle, output must receive at least more than one input meaning $f(a) = f(b), a \neq b$. Therefore this function can not be injective.
### 3b — why this needs finiteness, connected to $f(n)=2n$ on $\mathbb{Z}$

There is not concept of running out of room (slots) when the codomain is infinite. When we prove that a function is not injective, we show that there are more inputs than there are outputs which causes a collision when mapping inputs to outputs. When the codomain is infinite, this is not possible since there is no limit to how many slots there are.
