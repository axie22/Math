### R1 — contrapositive vs. contradiction: when and why

You reach for the contrapositive for "If $P$, then $Q$" statements when the negated conclusion (Not $Q$) gives you easier algebraic starting material to work with than the original premise. It is the "lighter" tool because it gives you a clear, specific target to reach (Not $P$), whereas contradiction forces you to wander blindly until you stumble into _any_ random mathematical impossibility.

### (a) f : ℤ → ℤ injective but not surjective

Injective, proof: Assume $f(a) = f(b)$, then $2a = 2b$, then we divide both sides by 2 and we get $a = b$

## Core 2 — contradiction

### (a) 3 | n² ⟹ 3 | n

Let's assume that $3 \nmid n$, then $n = 3k + 1$ or $n = 3k + 2$ for some $k \in \mathbb{Z}$. 
$$
\begin{aligned}
n &= 3k + 1 \\
n^2 &= (3k + 1)^2 \\
n^2 &= 9k^2 + 6k + 1 \\
&= 3(3k^2 + 2k) + 1 
\end{aligned}
$$
The expression can be simplified into $n ^2 = 3x + 1$ for some $x \in \mathbb{Z}$ which means when $n^2$ is divide by 3, there's a remainder of 1 so $3 \nmid n^2$ 
$$
\begin{aligned}
n &= 3k + 2 \\
n^2 &= (3k + 2)^2 \\
&= 9k^2 + 12k 3 + 1 \\
&= 3(3k^2 + 4k + 1) + 1
\end{aligned}
$$
The expression can be simplified into $n ^2 = 3x + 1$ for some $x \in \mathbb{Z}$ which means when $n^2$ is divide by 3, there's a remainder of 1 so $3 \nmid n^2$ 

We assumed $3 \nmid n$ which forced $n^2$ to take the form of $3m + 1$ in all possible cases so $3 \nmid n^2$
but our original statement was given $3 \mid n^2$ so we have reached a contradiction.
### (b) √3 is irrational

Let's assume that $\sqrt{3}$ is rational. Then it can be written as a fraction of some form with $\sqrt{3} = \frac{a}{b}$ where $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $b \neq 0$ and $\gcd(a, b) = 1$. 
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{3} &= \frac{a}{b} \\
3 &= (\frac{a}{b})^2 \\
3b^2 &= a^2
\end{aligned}
$$
We know that $a^2$ must be a factor of 3 so we can express $a = 3c$ for some $c \in \mathbb{Z}$
$$
\begin{aligned}
3b^2 &= 9c^2 \\
b^2 &= 3c^2
\end{aligned}
$$
This equation tells you that $b^2$ is a multiple of $3$ (since it equals $3 \times c^2$). By the rule we proved in part (a), since $3 \mid b^2$, it must also be true that $3 \mid b$. This means $b$ is a multiple of $3$.
